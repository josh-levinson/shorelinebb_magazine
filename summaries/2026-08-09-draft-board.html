How value is built

1 · Per-game rates. Games played range from 1 to 6, so season totals would just rank whoever showed up most. Rates level the field first.

2 · Reliability. Rates regress toward replacement level — the 25th percentile of the pool — by sample size. A 30-point average over three games is a weaker claim than the same average over six. The baseline is replacement level rather than the league mean on purpose: regressing toward the mean quietly rewards a below-average player for missing games, which punishes exactly the people who keep showing up.

adj = (GP·rate + 2·replacement) / (GP + 2)

3 · Composite. Each category is standardized across the pool, so a rebound and an assist carry comparable weight, then weighted below.

4 · Availability. Attendance against the 6 games of recorded stats, scaled into the final value.

×1.12 at 6/6 ×1.00 at 4/6 ×0.88 at 2/6 ×0.82 at 1/6

Teams forfeit when they can't field a roster, so showing up carries real value on its own — but it's deliberately tuned as a secondary factor. It moves players between tiers without letting a role player outrank an elite producer.

Data caveat

Teams have played 7 games but the league's stat table carries only 6 games of lines, so attendance is measured against 6. Per-event box scores are published empty, so every rate here comes from season totals divided by games played.

Reading the board. Ryan Florentine edges Jeremy McDonald for the top pick despite averaging five fewer points a game — McDonald's 30.0 comes from three appearances, Florentine's 24.8 from five, at a far better true-shooting clip. That's the model working as intended, not a quirk.
